Effects of structural interventions on quality of life among people ageing with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Boniface Mutatina, PhD

Uganda Christian University | Africa Centre for Systematic Reviews and Knowledge Translation, College of Health Sciences, Makerere University

Introduction

Background and rationale

With advancement in treatment and care, people living with HIV are now living longer. Despite this progress, aging comes with new physical, mental and psychosocial health challenges. As people living with HIV age, they become more vulnerable to multiple morbidities, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, musculoskeletal disorders, and neurocognitive disorders. They are also likely to experience social isolation, mental health challenges, income insecurity, and lack of social support, which may further compound the challenges of aging with HIV. Structural interventions to improve quality of life among people aging with HIV — including health system reforms, integrated chronic care models, social protection policies, adherence support structures, community empowerment, and capacity building — have been developed with positive outcomes. Nevertheless, the field of HIV and aging remains emerging, with a paucity of rigorous evidence on the effectiveness of existing structural interventions on quality of life.

Evidence on aging with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa remains fragmented. Most studies focus on stigma, clinical outcomes, and individual-level determinants of quality of life, with limited work on broader structural interventions. Existing reviews are largely narrative or scoping and do not provide pooled effect estimates. This systematic review and meta-analysis will address these gaps by synthesizing and quantifying the effects of structural interventions, generating comparable effect estimates across contexts, and integrating fragmented evidence into a coherent framework to inform policy and programming for healthy aging with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa.

This study is particularly relevant to Uganda, where an estimated 1.4 million adults are living with HIV. While prevalence among adults aged 15–49 years has declined, prevalence among adults aged ≥50 years (7.6%) remains higher than in younger age groups, reflecting a growing population of older adults living with HIV. Given limited health funding in Uganda and other LMICs, identifying high-impact structural interventions is essential for informing priority-setting in policy and program design.

The Ugandan disease environment also adds a distinct biological dimension that shapes the structural evidence base. APOL1 high-risk variants, which are prevalent in East African populations, interact with HIV to accelerate chronic kidney disease (CKD) and multimorbidity, potentially modifying the response to structural interventions. The SEARCH-CKD study documented a 12.5% CKD prevalence in eastern Uganda versus 3.9% in the southwest — a threefold within-country gradient that reflects this biological substrate. The live Uganda Christian University and Ukubona Uganda Renal Atlas (146 districts) provides the foundational platform to explore these effect modifiers, linking structural and biological determinants of quality of life in a way no prior review has attempted.

Methods

This review will be designed according to the recommendations of the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence and the Cochrane Handbook. Reporting will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

2.2.3. Types of interventions [revised — narrowed]

To maximize poolability and minimize unpoolable heterogeneity, eligible structural interventions will be bounded to three pre-specified domains:

  • A. Health-system delivery models: including integrated chronic care and differentiated service delivery models.
  • B. Social protection and economic strengthening: specifically cash transfer programs and food security initiatives.
  • C. Community-based adherence and retention systems: including peer support structures and community health worker models.

Heterogeneity will be explored through pre-specified subgroup analyses stratified by APOL1 high-risk genotype status (where reported) and HIV virological control status, leveraging the biological substrate documented in Ugandan cohorts.

Dissemination Plan

The findings of this systematic review will be disseminated through a targeted knowledge translation strategy designed to support uptake into national HIV programming, policy, and planning processes. Outputs will be published in open-access, peer-reviewed journals and presented at national and international conferences focused on HIV, ageing, and global health.

Specifically, outputs will be delivered directly to the Uganda National Planning Authority to inform the health module of the next National Development Plan, and to the Uganda AIDS Commission and Ministry of Health — both of which face increasing urgency to develop domestic evidence systems as external funding for HIV programming becomes less certain. Dissemination will be sustained through Ukubona's automated, AI-assisted living evidence synthesis infrastructure, functioning as a Policy Digital Twin: integrating machine-learning-supported screening, data extraction, and visualization to generate continuously updated systematic reviews in collaboration with Uganda Christian University.

Plan and Justification for Residence in the US

Justification for physical presence [fully rewritten]

My physical presence in the United States is essential to embed myself inside a living transnational research ecosystem anchored by Dr. Abimereki Muzaale, who has been at Johns Hopkins continuously since 2006. His JAMA 2014 study on living kidney donation safety, NIA K08 award on perioperative and long-term outcomes in older kidney donors, and ongoing GTPCI doctorate in clinical data science are the scientific and computational foundation for the Uganda Renal Atlas — the same infrastructure that underpins this systematic review. This is not a new or aspirational relationship: it is an active, revenue-generating, policy-engaged network that already connects Johns Hopkins to Uganda's National Planning Authority and Ministry of Health.

At the Johns Hopkins University Center on Aging and Health, I will gain direct access to NA-ACCORD and WIHS longitudinal cohort infrastructure and frailty-assessment methods developed over decades of HIV and aging research. These resources exist in a specific institutional and human configuration — shaped by twenty years of embedded Ugandan-American scientific presence — that cannot be interpreted or leveraged from Kampala.

In parallel, hands-on immersion with Ukubona LLC will transfer machine-learning pipelines for automated PRISMA workflows and citation-network analysis — methods developed through Dr. Muzaale's GTPCI doctoral training and operationalized through the LLC's existing SBIR work. These skills cannot be acquired through remote engagement alone. Upon my return, they will be institutionalized at Uganda Christian University and the Africa Centre for Systematic Reviews and Knowledge Translation.

This is not access to internet or library databases. It is the physical activation of a twenty-year Ugandan–American bridge that already connects Johns Hopkins to Uganda's National Planning Authority and Ministry of Health — and the transfer of a methodological infrastructure that will outlast the fellowship itself.

Relevancy to Fulbright's Goal

My research project advances the Fulbright goal of mutual understanding through a bidirectional exchange of knowledge and experience between Uganda/Sub-Saharan Africa and the United States. A key enabler of this work is collaboration with Ukubona LLC, a Ugandan-American research and data science platform anchored by a Ugandan-American surgeon-scientist who has been at Johns Hopkins since 2006, whose JAMA 2014 work defined modern informed consent for living kidney donation, and who is now completing a data-science doctorate while building the computational infrastructure that this fellowship will accelerate. My proposed research is modelled on this bidirectional collaboration. My host environment is therefore not a single institutional attachment but a sustained transnational partnership with two decades of scientific depth.
